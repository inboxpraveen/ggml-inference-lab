How the large labs do it

Before the recipe, the map, because the words get used loosely and the order matters. What follows is the pipeline as the labs themselves describe it in their papers, with the numbers they published. I've kept to primary sources; where the only source is a press article or a model card, the sources section says so.

Pretraining is next-token prediction on as much text as the budget allows. SmolLM2-135M saw {{SRC_SMOLLM2_TOKENS_135M}} tokens on {{SRC_SMOLLM2_GPUS}}s. Llama 3 405B saw {{SRC_LLAMA3_PRETRAIN_TOKENS_405B}} tokens on {{SRC_LLAMA3_H100_GPUS}} H100s, and its data mix was {{SRC_LLAMA3_PRETRAIN_MIX}}, so even a frontier model gives a twelfth of its reading to every language other than English put together. Nobody outside a lab repeats this stage. What you can repeat is the next one.

Continued pretraining is the same objective, the same loop, started from the pretrained weights and pointed at new text. When the new text is a language the model hasn't seen, it's called language adaptation, and it usually comes with a change to the tokenizer. Chinese-LLaMA added {{SRC_CHINESE_LLAMA_CHINESE_TOKENS_ADDED}} Chinese tokens to Llama's 32,000, which roughly halved the token count of a Chinese sentence, and continued on a {{SRC_CHINESE_LLAMA_PRETRAIN_CORPUS_7B}}. Swallow added {{SRC_SWALLOW_SUBWORDS_ADDED}} Japanese subwords to Llama 2 and continued for {{SRC_SWALLOW_CPT_TOKENS_TOTAL}} tokens at a {{SRC_SWALLOW_JA_EN_RATIO_USED}} Japanese-to-English ratio, and reported that the vocabulary expansion cut Japanese token counts by {{SRC_SWALLOW_JAPANESE_TOKEN_REDUCTION_PCT_INT}}% with no effect on most benchmarks. Sarvam's OpenHathi did the same for Hindi on Llama 2 in {{SRC_OPENHATHI_DATE}}. The other thing every one of these did is keep some of the original language in the mix, because a model trained only on the new language forgets the old one; the cleanest study of that, Ibrahim and colleagues in {{SRC_IBRAHIM_CPT_DATE}}, found that replaying {{SRC_IBRAHIM_CPT_REPLAY_WEAK_SHIFT_PCT}}% of the old data is enough for a mild distribution shift and {{SRC_IBRAHIM_CPT_REPLAY_STRONG_SHIFT_PCT}}% for a strong one, their example of a strong one being {{SRC_IBRAHIM_CPT_STRONG_SHIFT_EXAMPLE}}, and that the learning rate has to be warmed back up and decayed again rather than resumed where the original run left it.

Supervised fine-tuning is where the text predictor becomes a thing that answers. The model is shown prompts and good responses in a chat format and trained on the responses only. InstructGPT, the paper behind the first ChatGPT, did this on {{SRC_INSTRUCTGPT_SFT_PROMPTS}} prompts written and answered by {{SRC_INSTRUCTGPT_CONTRACTORS_N}} contractors. It's smaller than people expect. Airavata, the Hindi instruction model built on OpenHathi, used {{SRC_AIRAVATA_INDICINSTRUCT_EXAMPLES_FILTERED}} mostly machine-translated examples, kept only when a back-translation scored well enough.

Reinforcement learning comes after that, and it comes in flavours that are worth keeping apart because only one of them is available to a person with a laptop. InstructGPT's version is RLHF: contractors compare pairs of model answers ({{SRC_INSTRUCTGPT_RM_PROMPTS}} prompts' worth), a reward model is trained to predict their preferences, and the policy is optimised against the reward model with PPO on another {{SRC_INSTRUCTGPT_PPO_PROMPTS}} prompts. The human feedback is in the reward model, not in the loop; the loop runs against a learned proxy. Anthropic's Constitutional AI replaced the human comparisons for harmlessness with an AI judge applying {{SRC_CONSTITUTIONAL_AI_PRINCIPLES_COUNT}} written principles, which is RLAIF. Llama 3 ran {{SRC_LLAMA3_POST_TRAINING_ROUNDS}} rounds of collecting preferences, sampling from the latest model, fine-tuning on the best samples and then DPO, the paper's reason being that {{SRC_LLAMA3_DPO_VS_PPO}}. Qwen2.5's recipe is SFT, then DPO, then GRPO. All of these need either people or a strong judge model to produce the preference data, and that is the part a solo developer doesn't have.

The flavour you do have is the one DeepSeek made famous. DeepSeekMath introduced GRPO in {{SRC_DEEPSEEKMATH_DATE}}: sample a group of answers to each question ({{SRC_DEEPSEEKMATH_GRPO_GROUP_SIZE}} in the paper), score them, and use the group's own mean as the baseline instead of training a separate value model. DeepSeek-R1, a year later, showed that a rule-based reward, a script that checks whether the final answer matches the reference and whether the output has the right format, is enough to lift a base model on AIME 2024 from {{SRC_DEEPSEEK_R1_AIME2024_PASS1_R1ZERO_START_V1}}% to {{SRC_DEEPSEEK_R1_AIME2024_PASS1_R1ZERO_END_V1}}% pass@1 with no human preference data at all. Tülu 3 gave the idea its name, RLVR, reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. Two details from the R1 paper matter here. First, the pure-RL model (R1-Zero) was hard to read, so the released model started with a "cold start" supervised stage on {{SRC_DEEPSEEK_R1_COLD_START_EXAMPLES}}. Second, R1-Zero kept mixing languages inside its reasoning, so they added a language-consistency reward, the share of target-language words in the reasoning, and accepted a {{SRC_DEEPSEEK_R1_LANGUAGE_CONSISTENCY_EFFECT}} for the readability. A model that has just learned Hindi and is being asked to answer in it needs exactly that reward.

Which corrects the version of the pipeline I had in my head when I started, and probably the version most people carry around. The order is pretraining, then continued pretraining if the language or domain changes, then supervised fine-tuning, then RL; RL on a raw base model is the exception R1-Zero proved possible, not the rule. "Human feedback" means preference comparisons that train a reward model, which is a labelling operation, not something that happens while you train. And the human-free version, verifiable rewards, is not a compromise for people without annotators; it's the method the current reasoning models were built with.
